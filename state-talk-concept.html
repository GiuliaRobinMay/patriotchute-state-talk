01

The shape of it

Someone in your community clicks State Talk in the sidebar. They land on a page that asks for three things and nothing more: a name, a face, a state. Thirty seconds, no password to remember.

Then they're in their state's room. Text chat runs down the middle the way it always does. Above it sits a stage — a handful of circles for whoever is actually talking out loud right now, with a green ring around the person speaking. Most people just read and type. A few tap Ask to talk, get pulled up by the host, and the room becomes a call.

That's the whole product. The complexity isn't in the interface — it's in the two Mighty Networks limits underneath it, which shape everything. Those are section 03.

03

What Mighty Networks will and won't let us do

This is the part worth reading closely. Mighty Networks does support embedding an outside app, but without the Headless API the platform will not tell our app who is looking at it — and a couple of browser rules bite harder inside an embed than they would on a normal page. None of it is fatal. All of it changes the design.

Hard limit

The community can't tell us who the member is

Authenticated embeds — where a member's Mighty account flows into your app automatically — are tied to the Headless API on the Scale plan and above. On your plan, our app sees an anonymous browser and nothing else.

What we do insteadMembers make a lightweight account in the app: email → one-tap sign-in link → name, picture, state. That's the onboarding in screens 1 and 2, and it exists purely because this door is closed. It also means you own the profile data outright, which is not the worst consolation.

Hard limit

Microphones are blocked inside an embed

Browsers only grant mic access to an embedded frame if the hosting page explicitly permits it, one attribute at a time. We don't control the markup Mighty Networks generates around our embed, so we should assume that permission never arrives. Text chat is completely fine embedded. Live voice, realistically, is not.

What we do insteadChat lives inside the community. Tapping Ask to talk opens the same room in a new tab, where the mic works normally and the member is already signed in. One extra tap, and it dodges the problem entirely rather than betting on a workaround. Worth a 20-minute test on your actual network before we commit — if the permission does come through, we simply delete the extra tap.

Watch it

Sign-in has to survive inside a frame

Browsers now discard cookies set by an embedded app on a different domain, which is exactly our situation. Built the ordinary way, members would get logged out constantly and nobody would tell us why.

What we do insteadHold the session in the browser's own local storage rather than in cookies. Standard practice for embedded apps — it just has to be a decision made on day one, not a bug found in month three.

Watch it

The Mighty mobile apps may not render it

Custom embeds behave less predictably inside the native iOS and Android apps than they do on the web, and a good share of your members live in the app.

What we do insteadBuild the app mobile-first so it stands on its own at a real URL, and put a plain link to it next to the embed. If the frame renders in-app, wonderful. If it doesn't, the link still works, and a member never hits a dead end.

Solved

Keeping non-members out

If anyone with the URL can sign up, the room isn't really inside your community.

What we do insteadZapier's Mighty Networks connector fires whenever someone joins, leaves, or is tagged. That feed keeps an allow-list current, so sign-in only works for a live member's email address, and someone who leaves the community loses the rooms. No API plan required.

Solved

Putting it in the community

Mighty Networks supports external embeds on paid plans; on the free tier iframes go through an approval and allow-list step.

What we do insteadAdd it as its own Space with a custom URL. Nothing exotic — but do confirm your tier's embed rules before we build, since it decides whether the app is a Space or a prominent link.

06

Build it in three passes

Chat before voice, always. Chat is where the habit forms and it's a fraction of the work — if nobody comes back to type, voice would not have saved it.

Pass 1
~2 weeks

Text rooms, live in the community

The whole loop, minus the mic: sign in, make a profile, pick a state, chat in real time. Embedded and working.

  • Sign-in links, profiles, avatars, member allow-list via Zapier
  • Rooms for every state, live message delivery, history that persists
  • Embedded in a Space, with the mobile link beside it
  • Open to 3 or 4 states only — 51 empty rooms on launch day reads as a ghost town, and first impressions of a chat app are unforgiving
Pass 2
~2 weeks

The voice stage

Add the mic, and the moderation that has to arrive with it.

  • Stage and audience: hosts on mic, hand-raises, pull-up and mute controls
  • The pop-out tab that makes microphones legal
  • Auto-filter on messages, report button, host queue — screen 5
  • Per-state hosts, so you aren't personally moderating fifty rooms
Pass 3
ongoing

Make people come back

A chat room nobody remembers to open is a dead chat room. This pass is about the return trip.

  • Scheduled rooms — “Ohio, Thursdays at 8” beats hoping someone's around
  • Email when your state goes live, capped so it never becomes noise
  • Open the remaining states as demand shows up; merge the quiet ones into regions
  • If Mighty ships broader API access on your plan, swap our sign-in for theirs and delete screens 1 and 2
07

What I need from you

Four decisions. The first two change the build; the last two change how it feels to use.

Question 01

Which Mighty plan are you on?

It decides whether the app becomes its own Space with a proper embed, or a prominent link out. Worth ten minutes of checking before anything gets built.

Also worth doing: paste any public web page into an embed on your network and see what survives. That single test settles the two hard limits above.
Question 02

All fifty on day one, or a handful?

Fifty rooms across a community of a few thousand means most rooms have four people and no conversation. Quiet rooms are the single most common way this kind of app dies.

My lean: open four states, waitlist the rest. Scarcity reads as momentum; emptiness reads as failure.
Question 03

Open mic, or hosted rooms?

Anyone can grab a mic, or hosts run the stage and pull people up. This is a moderation decision dressed as a product one — open mics need someone watching, always.

My lean: hosted. Start with a stage and hand-raises; open it up per room once you know who your reliable hosts are.
Question 04

Does the chat scroll away, or stay?

Ephemeral rooms feel alive and lower the stakes of posting. Permanent history makes the room searchable and worth returning to — and gives you something to moderate against.

My lean: keep everything, show the last 30 days. Easy to shorten later; impossible to recover what you never stored.

Prototype is a front-end mock — the screens are real and clickable, nothing is wired to a server yet. Cost figures are August 2026 public pricing and should be re-checked at build time.
